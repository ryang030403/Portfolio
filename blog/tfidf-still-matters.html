TF-IDF still earns its place in the age of embeddings

Ryan Joshua George · October 2026 · 6 min read

When I started building my resume-screening engine, the obvious move was to reach for a large embedding model. Everyone does semantic search with dense vectors now. I went the other way and built it on TF-IDF, and the result surprised me: 88% shortlisting accuracy against a labelled validation set, with a full batch ranked against a job description in under 15ms.

This post is about why that worked, and when you should (and shouldn't) make the same call.

The problem is mostly lexical

Screening a resume against a job description is less about deep meaning than it looks. A posting asks for "Node.js", "Redis", and "Docker". A good resume says "Node.js", "Redis", and "Docker". The signal is in specific, rare-ish terms, and that is exactly what TF-IDF is designed to reward:

  • Term frequency rewards a resume for mentioning a skill repeatedly.
  • Inverse document frequency pushes down words every resume contains ("team", "experience", "worked") and lifts the distinctive ones.

Cosine similarity between the two vectors then gives you a score that is cheap to compute and easy to explain.

from sklearn.feature_extraction.text import TfidfVectorizer
from sklearn.metrics.pairwise import cosine_similarity

vec = TfidfVectorizer(ngram_range=(1, 2), stop_words="english", sublinear_tf=True)
matrix = vec.fit_transform([job_description, *resumes])
scores = cosine_similarity(matrix[0], matrix[1:]).ravel()
shortlist = [r for r, s in sorted(zip(resumes, scores), key=lambda x: -x[1])[:top_k] if s >= threshold]

Explainability is a feature

When a recruiter asks why a candidate was ranked low, "the cosine distance in a 1,536-dimension space was large" is not an answer. With TF-IDF I can point at the exact weighted terms that matched and the ones that were missing. For anything that affects people's jobs, that matters. You want a system whose decisions a human can audit and push back on.

Where it falls short

TF-IDF is not magic, and I hit its limits quickly:

  • Synonyms. "Postgres" and "PostgreSQL", or "k8s" and "Kubernetes", are different tokens. A small normalisation dictionary in the parsing step goes a long way.
  • Context. "Led a team migrating off Java" and "five years of Java" look similar to a bag of words.
  • Keyword stuffing. Sublinear TF scaling helps, but a lexical model can be gamed more easily than a semantic one.

The general lesson

Start with the simplest model that could plausibly work, measure it honestly on held-out data, and only add complexity when you can name the failure it fixes. In my case, a hybrid makes sense as the next step: use TF-IDF for fast, explainable first-pass ranking, then re-rank the top-K with embeddings to catch semantic matches the lexical pass misses.

A model you can run in milliseconds, test with a 6-case pytest suite, and explain to a non-engineer is often worth more than a slightly better one you can't.
