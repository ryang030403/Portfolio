I measured my resume screener on real data. It scored 55%.

Ryan Joshua George · October 2026 · 6 min read

I built a resume-screening engine on TF-IDF and cosine similarity. On my handful of sample resumes it looked great: the right candidates floated to the top, the tests passed, and it ranked a batch in milliseconds. Then I ran it against real, human-labelled data, and it scored 55%.

This post is about that number, and why finding it was the most useful thing I did on the project.

The setup

I used a public dataset of about 8,000 resume and job-description pairs, each labelled by a person as Good Fit, Potential Fit or No Fit. I treated "shortlist" as Good or Potential Fit. To keep the evaluation honest:

  • Resumes are ranked per job description, the same way the tool is used for real.
  • The shortlisting threshold is tuned on the training split only.
  • Accuracy is reported once, on a held-out test split of 1,759 pairs across 71 job descriptions the threshold never saw.
Held-out shortlisting accuracy : 55.0%
Majority-class baseline        : 48.7%
Precision / recall             : 56.5% / 52.9%
ROC AUC (ranking quality)      : 0.590  (0.5 = random)

So TF-IDF beats always guessing the most common label, but only by about six points. As a ranker, it's better than chance and not much more.

Why it struggles

TF-IDF rewards word overlap, so it works when the signal is lexical: the posting says "Redis" and the resume says "Redis". But a human deciding fit is looking at things word overlap can't see:

  • Seniority and depth. "Used Kubernetes once" and "ran our Kubernetes platform for three years" share the same keyword.
  • Context. "Migrated the team off Java" and "five years of Java" look alike as a bag of words.
  • Synonyms. "Postgres" and "PostgreSQL", or "k8s" and "Kubernetes", are different tokens.
  • Long documents. Real resumes are long and noisy, which dilutes the cosine similarity. My sample resumes were short and clean, which is exactly why they flattered the model.

What the small test set hid

My original sample data was four short resumes I could reason about by hand. A model can look perfect on data like that, because the data was written with the answer in mind. Speed fooled me the same way: ranking four tiny resumes took a few milliseconds, but ranking a batch of 25 real ones takes around 100ms. Neither number was wrong. They just weren't measuring the thing I cared about.

A test suite tells you the code does what you meant. An evaluation on real labelled data tells you whether what you meant was any good. You need both.

What TF-IDF is still good for

I'd still keep it around as a fast first pass. It's explainable: for every candidate I can show which required keywords matched and which were missing, which matters when the output affects someone's job application. But on this evidence it shouldn't be the only thing making the call.

What's next

The useful part of this project now is the evaluation harness, not the model. Since the 55% is measured reproducibly, I can swap in sentence embeddings or a trained classifier and see straight away whether they actually help, instead of guessing. The evaluation script and full results are in the repo. Run python evaluate.py and you should get the same numbers.
