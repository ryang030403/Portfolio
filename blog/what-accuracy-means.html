What 68.5% accuracy actually means

Ryan Joshua George · September 2026 · 5 min read

My chess analytics engine includes a logistic-regression model that predicts the winner from a board position. On 1,446 labelled positions it scores 68.5% accuracy on held-out data. Is that good? The honest answer is: it depends entirely on what you compare it to. That question is the most useful thing I've learned about evaluating ML models.

A number without a baseline is noise

Before celebrating any accuracy figure, ask what a trivial model would score. In chess, White wins somewhat more often than Black, so "always predict White" already beats a coin flip. A naive material-count rule (whoever has more pieces wins) does better still. Your model is only interesting by the margin it adds over those baselines.

I now report every model alongside at least two baselines:

  1. The majority-class predictor.
  2. The simplest domain heuristic a human would try first.

Held-out means held out

The quickest way to fool yourself is leakage. With chess positions it is easy to do by accident: several positions from the same game share an outcome, so if some land in training and others in test, the model can effectively memorise the game rather than learn anything about positions. 

from sklearn.model_selection import GroupShuffleSplit

# Split by game, not by position, so no game appears on both sides.
splitter = GroupShuffleSplit(n_splits=1, test_size=0.2, random_state=42)
train_idx, test_idx = next(splitter.split(X, y, groups=game_ids))

Expect a grouped split to score lower than a naive random split. That drop is the point: the lower number is the one you can trust.

Accuracy hides the interesting part

A win-probability model is useful because of its probabilities, not its hard labels. A model that says "55% White" and one that says "95% White" both count as correct if White wins, but they are making very different claims. So beyond accuracy, I look at:

  • Log loss, which punishes confident wrong answers.
  • Calibration: of all positions the model calls 70%, does White win about 70% of them?
  • Error by game phase: openings are close to even and hard to call, endgames much less so.

Logistic regression is a good choice here partly because it tends to be reasonably well-calibrated out of the box, and its coefficients are readable. You can see what the model thinks a pawn is worth.

Why this matters beyond chess

The same discipline applies to every AI feature people ship today, including LLM-powered ones. "Our classifier is 90% accurate" tells you nothing until you know the baseline, how the test set was built, and what the errors look like. Be the person on the team who asks those three questions. It's cheap, and it catches expensive mistakes.
